ONE character for the engine the story will run on. The engine is room-based
and the player acts through a menu: they can examine this person, see them
in a room, and talk to them about topics. You write what the player sees and
hears; code turns it into engine data.

What you write:
 - description: how this person looks and acts when the player examines
   them, as variants keyed to where their arc stands. This is where the arc
   becomes visible: the tells. A variant names a state and a direction
   (dana_trust up: she leaves the talkback key open), or a node after which

   the same sentence is read in every room they turn up in ("Dana is in the

 - topics: the CURATED things the player can talk to them about, the ones
   the story turns on: what they want, what they hold, what they know, what
   they are hiding, the people they are tied to. Three to eight for an arc
   character, two to five for a supporting one. For each: a label the menu
   shows ("the caller", "her last job"), known_after (a node id after which
   the player can ask; null if from the start), and what they say, as
   variants by state where their stance changes the answer. A topic may move
   a state when asked (rarely: asking her about the caller on air moves
   dana_trust down); most do not.
 - history (arc characters only): two or three sentences on what they bring
   into the story, concretely.
 - portrait: the character's visual portrait and physical silhouette profile for multimodal rendering and spatial grounding:
   * composition: portrait framing (bust or medium portrait, angle, posture)
   * subjects: [name/role, age, build, facial features, distinctive marks, gaze]
   * environment: clothing, fabric wear, accessories, silhouette
   * palette_mood: dominant color swatches, personal lighting, expression/mood
   * prompt_summary: dense prompt for multimodal character portrait (e.g. 16-bit retro pixel art, graphic novel woodcut, or atmospheric concept illustration)

Rules:
 - Write what they SAY in their own voice (the sketch gives it, with a
   sample line), as short speech with a little action. Under three
   sentences each.
 - NATURAL, GROUNDED SPEECH (NO APHORISMS OR RIDDLES):
   Characters must speak like real people grounded in their specific occupation, background, and social setting—NOT literary poets, brooding philosophers, or cryptic oracles.
   * NEVER write cryptic fortune-cookie epigrams or symmetrical definitions ("X is not a Y, it is a Z"):
     BAD (technical/sci-fi): "A reactor is not an engine. It is a heart."
     BAD (restrained/drama): "A list is not a goodbye. It is a count."
     BAD (historical/fantasy): "A crown is not iron. It is a leash."
   * Characters speak with practical intent: they deflect, complain, show dry humor, ask direct questions, give concrete operational details, or show nervous hesitation.
   * Subtext lives in what is avoided or unsaid beneath normal words, NOT in theatrical riddles.
 - NO REPETITIVE PROP MANIPULATION:
   Do not have characters endlessly perform symbolic actions with props (fiddling with holsters, wiping counters, tapping datapad screens, adjusting collars, turning keys, holding mugs) in dialogue exchanges as a substitute for real interpersonal interaction.
 - Stakes live in people, bodies, places and things. A ledger, a contract, a
   signature is a weak thing; use one only where the story is about it.
 - Only someone who knows the hidden truth (it is in the packet only if they
   do) may state it. Others may suspect, notice, avoid.
 - Do not invent new people. Refer to others by name or role as the packet
   does.
