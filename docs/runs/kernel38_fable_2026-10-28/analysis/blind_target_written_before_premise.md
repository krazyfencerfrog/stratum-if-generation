# kernel38: what a skilled IF author would build (written blind, before reading any pipeline output past step 2)

Kernel: Victorian country-house murder. Snowstorm, house cut off two days. Master dead in his locked study.
You are the NEW housekeeper, the only person who saw where everyone was that night. Seven guests, all
lying about something. Real clues and red herrings, a fair solution, and the player can accuse the wrong person.

## The genre's contract (what the player who typed this expects)

1. A closed circle: the snow means the killer is in the house and nobody can leave. Time pressure: the thaw /
   the police are coming, and until then the killer can act again.
2. A locked room with a FAIR mechanical explanation the player could work out.
3. Seven suspects, each with a secret, each lie a red herring except one. Peeling the lies IS the game.
4. The "you" is the housekeeper: below the guests' notice (they talk in front of her), with the keys and the
   servants' passages (she can go anywhere), with the household's eyes (butler, maids, cook) as a second
   source, AND the obvious suspect (new, no history here, holds the keys, found the body).
5. A denouement: a gathering where you accuse, and the accusation can be WRONG, with consequences.
6. Deadline / The Witness / Christie: investigation is the loop (explore, examine, question, confront with
   evidence); the story's events (second attack, evidence destroyed, a guest tries to leave) are on a clock.

## The player character (she needs her own stake or the investigation is a chore)

Ada Pell, housekeeper, three weeks in post. Why she investigates instead of waiting: (a) the keys are hers, so
she hangs if nobody else does; (b) she is the only one who knows the night's true geography, and every guest's
account contradicts it somewhere; (c) her own secret: her reference is forged, and she took the post because
her sister served here two years ago and left "in disgrace" and has not written since. The dead man knew.
Want: get through the two days without being accused. Need: to stop protecting herself and protect the house
(the living). Open to the player: whether she uses what she learns to protect, to trade, or to escape.

## The engine (what makes events happen without the player)

- Pressure: the thaw. The road clears in roughly 36 hours of story time; the police arrive then. Everything the
  killer must do (recover the will draft, silence the maid who saw them on the landing, shift suspicion onto
  the housekeeper) happens on that clock whether or not you act.
- Hidden truth (the fair solution): the master was not killed in the locked study. He was poisoned at dinner
  (the digitalis from his own heart medicine, in the brandy he alone drank). He locked himself in after dinner
  as he did every night, to finish rewriting his will, and died at the desk. The LOCKED ROOM IS THE RED
  HERRING. The question is who had the medicine, who knew about the new will, and who refilled the decanter.
- The killer: the widow's brother (guest 3), who manages the estate's money and has been embezzling; the new
  will would have put the estate under a trustee. He has an ally who does not know she is one: the widow
  (guest 1), who hid the half-written will because it disinherited her stepson, and so has been destroying the
  evidence that would convict her brother.
- Opposition: the killer, but mostly the HOUSE: seven people with seven reasons to keep you from the truth.
  Each lie protects something that is not the murder, and exposing it costs someone.

## The seven guests, their lie, what it hides, and the clue that opens it

1. The widow, Lady Hale. Lie: she was in bed by eleven. Truth: she went into the study after he died (she has
   the spare key from the master's dressing room), found the will draft, took it, and burned part of it.
   Clue: ash in her bedroom grate; the housekeeper SAW her on the stairs at half past eleven.
2. The stepson, Captain Hale. Lie: he was in the gun room cleaning the guns. Truth: he was in the stable with
   the groom's daughter. Clue: straw on his boots; the groom's daughter's story; the gun room cold, fire unlit.
3. The widow's brother, Mr Thorne. Lie: he and the master parted on good terms after port. Truth: they
   quarrelled about the accounts; the master had found the shortfall. Clue: the ledger in the estate office with
   two pages cut out; the butler heard raised voices; the brandy decanter wiped clean (the only clean thing on
   the tray).
4. The doctor, Dr Marsh. Lie: he examined the body and found a plain heart failure. Truth: he recognised the
   signs of digitalis and said nothing, because he prescribed it and his own dosing had been careless (an
   earlier patient died). Clue: the medicine bottle at a level that does not match the dosage card; the
   doctor's bag has a second bottle.
5. The old friend, Colonel Ames. Lie: he came for the shooting. Truth: he came to ask for money; his
   daughter's debts. Clue: a letter in the study blotter (mirror-writing); the Colonel has not touched a gun.
6. The daughter of the house, Miss Hale. Lie: nothing happened at dinner. Truth: she is secretly engaged to the
   doctor and the master had forbidden it; she was in the doctor's room that night. Clue: her hairpin in the
   doctor's room; the maid who turned down the beds.
7. The guest nobody invited, Mrs Verity, "a cousin". Lie: her name and her connection. Truth: she is the
   master's natural daughter by a woman in the village, invited BY HIM to be named in the new will. She is the
   one person with no motive and the one person every other guest wants to blame. Clue: the letter in her
   room in the master's hand; the vicar's register.

The housekeeper's own night-map is the master key: she saw (1) the widow on the stairs, (2) the Captain
cross the yard, (3) Thorne leave the study last, (4) the doctor's door open late, (5) the Colonel in the
library, (6) Miss Hale's room empty, (7) Mrs Verity's lamp lit all night. Each guest's first account breaks
against one of these, and the player can CHOOSE which contradiction to press and in front of whom.

The staff: Pryce the butler (loyal to the dead man, cold to you, the only one who knows about the digitalis
routine; wins over if you protect the household's name), Nell the between-maid (saw Thorne at the decanter;
frightened; the second victim if you do not get her story out or protect her), Cook (knows what everyone ate
and drank: the fair-play source for the poisoning), the groom.

## Structure and rhythm

- Night 1 (prologue, short, linear): the body found. You are the one who unlocks the door with your own key
  in front of the butler. Everyone's first account, the geography fixed in your head. The doctor's verdict:
  heart. The widow asks you to keep the house running "as if nothing has happened".
- Day 2 morning (slow, exploratory): the house to run and a house to search. Seven accounts to collect, the
  study to go over (the locked-room red herrings: the window, the chimney, the key in his pocket), the
  servants' hall as a second source. Discoveries are OPTIONAL and VARIED: documents, physical traces,
  overheard talk, the staff's gossip, the house's history.
- Day 2 afternoon (first acceleration, events): the will draft is missing from the study (the widow), the
  ledger pages are cut (Thorne), Nell asks to speak to you and is interrupted. Thorne suggests to the widow
  that the new housekeeper's references be checked (your secret on the clock).
- Day 2 night (crisis): the second attack: Nell is found at the foot of the back stairs. Whether she is dead,
  hurt or safe depends on what you did (moved her to your room; got her story in front of Pryce; or nothing).
  If she dies, the house turns on you (your keys, your passages).
- Day 3 morning (climax): the thaw. Police in two hours. The drawing room gathering. You accuse, or you
  don't. The accusation has to be MADE OF what you found: the engine should check which evidence you hold.
- Endings (worlds, not labels): (a) right accusation with the decanter, the ledger and Nell's word: Thorne
  is held, the widow's part comes out or you keep it (your choice, with a cost either way); (b) right person,
  thin evidence: Thorne turns it on you, the widow backs him, the police take you; later vindication
  depends on whether Pryce speaks; (c) wrong person: the innocent's secret is exposed in front of everyone
  (the engagement, the Colonel's debts, Mrs Verity's birth), Thorne thanks you, and that night the house
  loses something else; (d) no accusation: the police arrive to a house where the only stranger holds the
  keys. Variants on every ending: Nell alive or dead; your own secret kept, traded or exposed; who stands with
  you (Pryce, Miss Hale, Mrs Verity).

## What makes it feel like IF

- A map worth walking: the study, the library, the dining room, the drawing room, the hall and stairs, the
  gun room, the estate office, the guests' corridor and three or four bedrooms, the servants' hall, the
  kitchen, the housekeeper's room, the back stairs and passages, the stable yard. Rooms change with time
  (the grate cold, then swept; the decanter there, then gone).
- Examine everything; most things talk about a person or the house's past, a few are clues.
- Every guest can be asked about every other guest and every clue; what they say changes as their lie cracks.
- Evidence is an inventory: a found thing can be shown to someone. Showing the right thing to the right person
  is how lies open, and showing it to the wrong person is how evidence disappears.
- The player chooses what to DO with a secret (keep it, use it, tell the widow, tell Pryce): these are the
  moral choices, and they move who trusts you.

## The yardstick, then: what each stage must produce for this to be good

- Premise: a fair hidden truth with a mechanism; a killer with a reason; a second victim on a clock; seven
  distinct lies with seven distinct costs of exposure; a protagonist with her own secret and a reason to act.
- Structure: investigation loop with an accelerating event clock, a crisis before the denouement, an
  accusation whose outcome is computed from evidence held and whom you told what.
- Choices: which lie to press, in front of whom; what to do with each secret; whom to protect; whom to
  accuse. Consequences telegraphed by the people themselves ("if that comes out, she is finished").
- NPCs: each with a want, a lie, a thing they would do if cornered, and a different stance toward you by the
  end; at least Pryce, Nell, the widow and Miss Hale with arcs that resolve differently by path.
- Endings: earned by evidence and by what you protected.
